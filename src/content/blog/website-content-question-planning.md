When you're comparing website cost in Malaysia, it helps to know what the site needs to explain before you ask for a quote. Start with the questions customers already ask. They can help you decide what belongs on the main page, what needs its own page, and what can wait.

A recent video by Edward Sturm suggests searching for your main service plus a town, then checking Google's "People also ask" questions for ideas. That's a handy brainstorm. It isn't search-volume data, and every question doesn't need its own page.

> TL;DR: Collect the questions customers already ask (WhatsApp, calls, PAA suggestions), then place each answer where it belongs — short answers on the service page, pre-contact worries in its FAQ, and a separate page only when the question has different intent plus real detail behind it.

## Start with real customer questions

Take an air-conditioning service in Shah Alam as an example. A potential customer might ask whether the company covers their area, what's included in a service, or how to request a quotation. Those are examples to help plan a page, not claims about what Shah Alam residents search most.

Use Google's suggestions as a starting point, then compare them with questions from WhatsApp chats, calls and quotation requests. If the same point keeps coming up before someone is ready to book, it may belong on the website.

## Choose the right place for each answer

A quick answer about service areas or what's included can sit on the service page. A short concern that comes up just before someone contacts you can go in that page's FAQ.

Give a question its own page when it needs a proper explanation, has a different customer intent, and you have useful detail to add. A separate page might make sense for a distinct service with its own process, photos or requirements. Don't create one page for every wording variation. Google's [SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide) says site owners don't need to anticipate every variation of a search; make the page useful for the reader instead.

## Turn the list into a simple page plan

For each question, write down:

- The customer's question, in their own words.
- The information they need before deciding.
- Any real details that support the answer, such as your process, service area or project photos.
- What they can do next, such as WhatsApp for a quotation.

This gives a designer something more useful than "make it look professional." It also helps you avoid paying to build pages before you know what they need to say.

After the site is live, check Google Search Console's [Performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/10268906) to see which queries brought impressions and clicks to your pages. That's evidence about how your site is appearing in Google, not a promise of traffic or enquiries.

## Common questions

### How many customer questions should I put on a website?

Start with the questions that come up repeatedly in real conversations. Put the short answers where customers need them. You can add more later if the site or customer feedback shows a gap.

### Should every "People also ask" question get a separate page?

No. Use those questions as prompts. A related question may fit in an FAQ, while a question with a different purpose may need its own page. Add a separate page only when you can answer it well.

### Does planning the content make a website cheaper?

It can make the scope clearer before you request a quote. It doesn't automatically reduce the work or price. The final cost depends on the pages and features you agree to build.

### Do I need a CMS to update website content?

Only if you want to make those changes yourself. Professional + Lite CMS includes an admin login for basic content updates. The right choice depends on who will maintain the site and how often it changes.
